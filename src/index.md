about:
  id: about
  title: About
  image: /assets/images/christian-scharen-photo.webp
  text: >
    Christian Scharen is the Gordon Braatz Chair and Professor of Practical
    Theology at the Lutheran School of Theology at Chicago. He is known for
    groundbreaking work on theology, ethics and ethnography, helping launch the
    new field of theological ethnography. A founder of the international Network
    for Ecclesiology and Ethnography (with Prof. Pete Ward), he has given
    lectures, collaborated on research projects, and mentored students around
    the world. His most recent research and writing critically engages histories
    of settler colonialism and violence against Indigenous peoples in the
    Americas. He is author of two forthcoming books dealing with America's—and
    his family's—pioneer past. *After Laura: Notes on Inheriting a Pioneer
    Legacy in America* will be published by TBA in 2028 *Custer Died for Our
    Sins: A Practical Theology of Repair* will be published by Fortress Press in
    August 2027. He has written more than a dozen other books and scores of
    articles, chapters, and reports on a wide variety of topics. An ordained
    Lutheran pastor, he received his PhD in Religion from Emory University.
    Raised in Montana, he currently lives in Chicago with his wife, Pierrette,
    and French bulldog Walt. They have three adult sons, Isaiah, Finn, and Owen.

after_laura:
  id: after-laura
  info: Forthcoming Memoir
  title: After Laura
  image: /assets/images/dad-and-chris.webp
  text:
    - article: >
        When Christian Scharen’s father died, he knew he finally would have to
        face his complicated relationship to his father—and his father’s
        **famous American pioneer story**. His childhood in Montana and Wyoming
        was formed by the pioneer tales of Laura Ingalls Wilder, but he’d never
        grappled with the fact that they were his family stories, too.
    - article: >
        He resolved to drive west from Pepin, Wisconsin, where his
        great-grandmother Lottie and her double-cousin Laura were both born in
        1867. He stopped to visit the land where the Ingalls homesteaded as well
        as sites important to the Indigenous peoples whose land it was prior to
        the pioneers’ arrival.
    - article: >
        Along the way, he discovered the awful truth of land theft and genocide
        perpetrated against Native Nations, a truth his own family stories
        elide. At turns loving and heart-wrenching, this book looks for—and
        finds— hard won healing with a father, even as its explores repair
        required for healing a nation’s relationship with its original occupants
        and with the land itself.
